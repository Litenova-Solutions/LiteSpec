# Intent Hierarchy Evidence

The Intent layer holds the reasons a product exists, such as the product, its goals, problems, features, requirements, constraints, research, decisions, and flows. This research record checks which links connect Intent records, which of them LiteSpec requires and checks, and how other tools and the largest consumer group their requirements. The largest consumer is a project that uses LiteSpec. It matters because 719 of 928 requirements in that consumer name no origin, and the current rules do not count that as a defect.

## Findings

Above the requirements, LiteSpec's hierarchy rests on rationale prose rather than on required or checked links. In the largest consumer, 719 of 928 requirements name no origin. The record states that every enforced Intent check passes in that consumer.

The schema requires only 4 Intent links, and a check enforces each of them. They are `product.audience`, `flow.actor`, a requirement's `except` entry to its `fallback`, and `decision.decides` on product decisions. The provision and the check require `decision.decides` even though the schema leaves it optional. Every other link above the requirements is optional in the schema, is mentioned only in the provision's rationale, and is unchecked. The record gives high confidence for this claim, because it read each cell from the schema, the provision text, and the check table.

One layer below Intent, LiteSpec already applies the principle that every record points up. A use case with neither `serves` nor `fulfills` fails `record-link-missing` at tier G. LiteSpec applies the principle nowhere inside Intent. The record gives high confidence for this claim.

The central measured gap is that 719 of 928 requirements name neither `feature` nor `basedOn`. Of 928 requirements, 56 name a `feature`, 164 name `basedOn`, and 11 carry both. The absence of `feature` alone is not a defect, because the field is defined narrowly. It names the feature that carries the requirement in a named phase, so a built requirement correctly carries none. The record gives no confidence for this claim.

The combined absence is still not a conformance defect today either. The provision puts the expectation in its rationale. Its statement and its check require only the identifier, `kind`, `statement`, and dated applied values. So the record counts the 719 as specification-tolerated traceability defects. They are the fault of LiteSpec's missing obligation and missing check, not of the consumer's authors. The record gives no confidence for this claim.

One measured signal shows that the anchor already matters in practice. Of 150 requirements that nothing below fulfills, all 100 without a `feature` appear as known `requirement-fulfillment-missing` findings. None of the 50 with a `feature` produced such a finding. The record gives no confidence for this claim.

Requirements follow 3 different groupings, so one feature's story spreads over 3 files. A requirement page groups commitments by area. A feature groups an offer under a problem. A flow groups steps under a goal. The parent of a requirement is its feature, the file that holds it is the requirement page, and the flow that walks it sits under a third record. The record gives no confidence for this claim.

The 3 agent-driven peers group records by the unit the author writes. GitHub Spec Kit keeps one spec file per feature, with user scenarios, numbered functional requirements, and success criteria. OpenSpec keeps one spec per capability, with Requirement blocks and Scenario examples. Kiro keeps one requirements file per spec, with user stories and EARS acceptance criteria. The record gives high confidence for this claim, because it read the shape from each tool's own concept pages.

The largest consumer shows that authors follow the page, not the parent. Its 719 requirements name no origin. All 112 goal sentences are distinct, and none contains a goal record's title. The record gives high confidence for this claim, because the consumer counts are measured, not sampled.

The record proposes that each feature hold its requirements as entries. The identifier `requirement/<feature>.<name>` already names the feature. A requirement whose feature segment differs from its file's feature is an identifier-invalid finding. A reader cannot see what was promised for one offer from one place. The parent rule also produces hundreds of missing-parent findings that authors did not see as mistakes. The record gives no confidence for this proposal.

A requirement has 3 origin fields with a precedence rule, where one ordered list would suffice. The origin is the first `features` entry, otherwise the first `basedOn` entry, otherwise a decision that names the requirement in `decides`. A goal cannot be a requirement's reason at all. The record gives no confidence for this claim.

Goal-oriented practice refines goals directly into requirements. KAOS refines goals into requirements, and impact mapping traces deliverables to a goal through an actor and an impact. With the feature as the parent, the precedence rule serves no purpose. So the record proposes that a requirement name its reasons in one `cites` list. The list holds any of goal, problem, feature, constraint, research, and decision, in no order of precedence. A requirement that cites nothing fails `requirement-cites-missing` at tier G. The record gives no confidence for this proposal.

Fulfillment and constraint answers each get one home. A flow alone clears `requirement-fulfillment-missing`, because the checker counts rule, use case, and flow as fulfilling. The record proposes that only Behavior records fulfill a requirement. Those are the rule, use case, reaction, job, notification, and setting. A flow names the requirements it shows in `cites`. The record gives no confidence for this proposal.

Authors write a constraint's answer twice, once as prose under What It Requires and once as requirement entries that cite the constraint. Of 29 constraints, 5 are cited by none. So the record proposes that What It Requires become a generated region that lists the requirements citing the constraint. A constraint with `applies` set to true that no requirement cites is `constraint-unreached` at tier G. Volere supports this shape by treating a mandated constraint as a requirement with its own rationale and fit criterion. The record gives no confidence for these claims.

Names should take their outside meaning. LiteSpec's `kind` for business requirements holds dated values, such as a receipt threshold or a price. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 names the Business Requirements Specification as the business or mission level above stakeholder requirements. Wiegers and Beatty use business requirements for why the organization builds the system. So the record proposes that the kind become `policy`, and that the constraint kind gain `legal` for laws. The record gives no confidence for this proposal.

The word flow also covers scheduled work that nobody walks. Story mapping and use-case narratives walk a person through activities toward a goal. So the record proposes that flows walk a person only. Scheduled promises become requirements that jobs fulfill. A flow drops `aim`, because its 112 sentences match no goal title and differ from `serves`. A step's sentence becomes `statement`, and its failure text becomes `failure`. The record gives no confidence for these proposals.

The Intent page never says why each record type exists or when not to use it. It has 11 similar types, each with one line and no write-when, skip-when, or familiar name. Each comparable method teaches a chain of questions. Impact mapping asks why, who, how, and what, and the opportunity solution tree asks about outcome, opportunity, and solution. So the record proposes a Concepts section that opens with a table of the questions Intent answers. The record gives no confidence for this proposal.

Tracing above the requirements costs more than it returns today. Teams skip tracing that costs effort without a direct benefit, and the links stop matching the records. The record proposes that strict parents stay where checks read them, which are the requirement and the flow step. Links above them stay advisory. The record gives no confidence for this proposal.

Goals connect weakly to the records below them. All 40 features carry `solves`, and 12 features serve all 8 goals through 16 `serves` links. The gap is that 28 of 40 features name no goal. Flows name their goal as prose in all 112 cases. The schema requires a string, the provision requires the goal in words, and the Links table holds no flow-to-goal edge. The record gives high confidence for the `solves` count, because the counts are measured. It gives no confidence for the flow claims.

A migration warning follows from the measurements. All 112 goal sentences are distinct, and none contains a goal record's title. So no mechanical mapping from the sentences to the 8 goals exists. The record gives no confidence for this warning.

The record tolerates several other record groups, and leaves some by design. Of 29 constraints, 5 are cited by no `basedOn`, and of 29 research records, 15 are. No rule requires citation, so they remain available as bases. The record notes that 15 of 29 suggests authors write research faster than decisions cite it. All 3 product decisions decide, with 2, 65, and 4 targets. The 71 technical decisions carry no `decides` by design, and they govern code instead. Of them, 8 draw `decision-unimplemented`, and 15 draw the advisory `decision-basis-missing`. The record gives no confidence for these claims.

The record also tolerates other cases. No feature solves one of 30 problems, which the record treats as deliberate. Nothing names exactly one actor, `actor/report-recipient`, which the record calls a genuine but minor orphan. No record cites 14 of 164 intent-layer terms, and the record tolerates that. The record could not judge requirement-page container conformance from the model, because the builder emits zero `requirement-page` records. That needs the consumer validation run. The record gives no confidence for these claims.

The investigation tested its recommendation clause by clause. It supports the requirement origin clause with one amendment. The wording keeps `feature` and `basedOn` distinct, the checker reports the found anchor as one finding, and a requirement settled by a decision counts as anchored. The investigation supports the feature clause for `solves`, because all 40 features already comply. `serves` should stay advisory, because with only 8 goals, forcing one goal per feature produces vague claims. The expectation should attach to dated features, where it informs sequencing. The record gives no confidence for these verdicts.

The record argues against the flow goal clause as a replacement and supports it as an added optional link. The sentence in words lets a reader judge a flow chapter without opening a goal page. The record supports the advice-first checks with a written promotion trigger. The trigger has 3 parts: zero advisory findings on merged records, checks added one slice at a time, and promotion through the waiver mechanism with an end date. The record gives no confidence for these verdicts.

# Intent Hierarchy Evidence

## Answer

This record distils the Intent layer hierarchy investigation of 2026-10-30 and the outside review of the same date into the claims this release relies on. Counts below were measured over the largest consumer's built model on 2026-10-30, holding 21,548 records with 928 requirements. The outside review read ten findings numbered F1 to F10 against comparable tools and measured consumer counts. The web sources listed in this record's metadata carry the review's read date of 2026-10-30; Notes on Sources states what was re-read and what was carried.

Only four Intent links are required by schema, stated as obligations, and enforced by a check at the same time: `product.audience`, `journey.actor`, a requirement `except` entry to its `fallback`, and `decision.decides` on product decisions, the last by provision plus check despite an optional schema. Every other link above the requirements is optional in the schema, mentioned only in provision Rationale, and unchecked. One layer down LiteSpec already accepts the every-record-points-up principle: a use case with neither `serves` nor `fulfills` fails `record-link-missing` at tier G. The principle was simply never applied inside Intent. Confidence is high: each cell was read from the schema, the provision text, and the check table.

The central measured gap is requirements with no origin: 719 of 928 name neither `feature` (56 do) nor `basedOn` (164 do, 11 carry both). Absence of `feature` alone is not a defect, because the field is defined narrowly as the feature that carries the requirement in a named phase, so a built requirement correctly carries none. The combined absence is still not a conformance defect today, because the provision puts the expectation in its Rationale while its Statement and check require only identifier, `kind`, `statement`, and dated applied values. The 719 are therefore specification-tolerated traceability defects, chargeable to LiteSpec's missing obligation and missing check rather than to the consumer's authors. One measured signal shows the anchor is already load-bearing in practice: of 150 requirements fulfilled by nothing below, the 100 without a `feature` all appear as known `requirement-fulfillment-missing` findings, while none of the 50 with a `feature` produced such a finding.

Requirements are grouped three different ways, so one feature's story is spread over three files. A requirement page groups commitments by area, a feature groups an offer under a problem, and a journey groups steps under a goal, while the parent of a requirement is the feature, the file holding it is the requirement page, and the journey that walks it sits under a third record. The three agent-driven peers all group by the unit the author writes in: GitHub Spec Kit keeps one spec file per feature holding the user scenarios, numbered functional requirements, and success criteria together; OpenSpec keeps one spec per capability holding Requirement blocks with Scenario examples; Kiro keeps one requirements file per spec with user stories and EARS acceptance criteria. The largest consumer shows authors follow the page, not the parent, and the 719 requirements above name no origin while all 112 goal sentences are distinct and none contains any goal record's title. A reader cannot answer what was promised for one offer, and how a person lives it, from one place, and the parent rule produces hundreds of missing-parent findings the authors did not experience as mistakes. Each feature therefore holds its requirements as entries, since the identifier `requirement/<feature>.<name>` already names it, and a requirement whose feature segment differs from its file's feature is an identifier-invalid finding. Confidence is high: the peer shape is read from the tools' own concept pages, and the consumer counts are measured, not sampled.

A requirement has three origin fields with a precedence rule, where one ordered list would do. The origin is the first features entry, else the first basedOn entry, else a decision that names the requirement in decides, and a goal cannot be a requirement's reason at all. Goal-oriented practice refines goals directly into requirements: KAOS refines goals into requirements, and impact mapping traces deliverables to a goal through actor and impact. With the feature as parent, the precedence rule has no work left, so a requirement names its reasons in one cites list holding any of goal, problem, feature, constraint, research, and decision, in no order of precedence. A requirement that cites nothing is requirement-cites-missing at tier G. The container follows the same fix: requirements become entries in their feature file, and one sentence on the Intent page says why requirements are entries.

Fulfilment and constraint answers each get one home. A journey alone clears requirement-fulfillment-missing today, because the checker counts rule, use case, and journey as fulfilling, so only Behavior records fulfil a requirement from here: rule, use case, reaction, job, notification, and setting, while a journey names the requirements it shows in cites. A constraint's answer is written twice, as prose under What It Requires and as requirement entries citing the constraint, and five of 29 constraints in the largest consumer are cited by none, so What It Requires becomes a generated region listing the requirements that cite the constraint, and a constraint with applies true that no requirement cites is constraint-unreached at tier G. Volere supports the shape by treating a mandated constraint as a requirement with its own rationale and fit criterion, so the response stays measurable.

Names follow outside meaning. LiteSpec's kind business is dated values such as a receipt threshold or a price, while ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 names the Business Requirements Specification as the business or mission level above stakeholder requirements, and Wiegers and Beatty use business requirements for why the organization builds the system, so the kind becomes policy and the constraint kind gains legal for laws. A system journey stretches the word journey over scheduled work nobody walks: story mapping and use-case narratives walk a person through activities toward a goal, so journeys walk a person only, scheduled promises become requirements that jobs fulfil, a journey drops aim since its 112 sentences match no goal title and drift from serves, and a step's sentence becomes statement with its failure text as failure.

The Intent page never says why each record type exists or when not to use it: eleven similar types with one line each and no write-when, skip-when, or familiar name. Each comparable method is taught as a chain of questions, Why, Who, How, What in impact mapping and outcome, opportunity, solution in the opportunity solution tree, so the Concepts section opens with a table of the questions Intent answers, with Question, Record, Write It When, Skip It When, and Familiar As columns. Tracing above the requirements costs more than it returns today: teams skip tracing for effort without direct benefit and links going stale. Strict parents stay where checks read them, the requirement and the journey step, and links above them stay advisory.

Features split cleanly. All 40 features carry `solves`, and all eight goals are served by 16 `serves` links from 12 features, so the top of the chain is intact; the gap is that 28 of 40 features name no goal. Journeys name their goal as prose in 112 of 112 cases, by design: the schema requires a string, the provision requires the goal in words, and the Links table holds no journey to goal edge. A migration warning is measured: all 112 goal sentences are distinct and none contains any goal record's title, so no mechanical mapping to the eight goals exists.

The remaining populations are tolerated or by design. Five of 29 constraints and 15 of 29 research records are cited by no `basedOn`; no rule requires citation, so they form a basis pool, though 15 of 29 suggests the pool is written faster than decisions consume it. All three product decisions decide (2, 65, and 4 targets); the 71 technical decisions carry no `decides` by design and govern code instead, where eight already draw the checked `decision-unimplemented` finding and 15 draw advisory `decision-basis-missing`. One of 30 problems is solved by no feature, deliberately left without one. Exactly one actor, `actor/report-recipient`, is named by nothing at all, a genuine but minor orphan. Fourteen of 164 intent-layer terms are cited by no record, which is tolerated. Requirement-page container conformance could not be judged from the model, because the builder emits zero `requirement-page` records; it needs the consumer validation run, not this record.

The investigation tested the recommendation clause by clause. The requirement origin clause is supported with one amendment: keep `feature` and `basedOn` distinct in the wording, report the anchor found as one finding, and count a decision-settled requirement as anchored. The feature clause is supported for `solves`, since all 40 features already comply, but `serves` should stay advisory: with only eight goals, forcing one goal per feature manufactures vague claims, so the expectation should attach where it informs sequencing, on dated features. The journey goal clause is argued against as a replacement and supported as an added optional link, because the sentence in words is what lets a reader judge a journey chapter without opening a goal page. Advice-first checks are supported with a written promotion trigger: zero advisory findings on merged records, checks landing one slice at a time, and promotion through the waiver mechanism with an end date.

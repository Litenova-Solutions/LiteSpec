The Intent Layer

Intent is where a product states why it exists and what it promises, in words a decider can approve. This deck explains every Intent record by building one product with it: Lantern, a small hotel booking service.

Before you start

How to Read This Deck

The deck is for people who will write, approve or review Intent records: deciders, product people, and the agents and developers who build from them. You need no LiteSpec knowledge; each term is explained where it first appears.

After reading, you can

  • say which Intent record answers which question
  • write each record for a real product
  • tell when a record is not needed
  • read what the checks report about Intent

The running example

Lantern lets travellers book hotel rooms. Every slide shows the record Lantern writes, so you can follow one product from its brief to its checks.

The story behind it

Ana searches Lisbon for 12 to 15 May 2027, picks a double room shown at EUR 384.50 for three nights, pays, and receives her confirmation. Everything Lantern promises about that walk is Intent.

Every Lantern record in this deck exists as a file in the published example. litespec consumer passes on its 40 files under docs/, and litespec model check exits 0; the checks slide shows what that means. Names, figures and the cited laws are invented for teaching.
Where Intent sits

Four Record Layers, Each Answering One Question

LiteSpec describes a product as records: small Markdown files with a JSON block, each of one type. The records form four layers. Each layer is written from the one above it, and only Intent is decided by people.

Where Intent sits

Intent Says What Is Promised, Never How It Works

An Intent record is written in product words a decider can approve without reading code. Behavior points up at Intent; Intent never names a Behavior record. Keeping the two apart lets the business change a promise without touching the design, and the design change without reopening the promise.

TopicIntent says (product words)Behavior says (design words)
Holding a roomA room the traveller starts to book is held for them for 10 minutes.Use case bookings.create-hold, state Held, refusal code bookings.room-gone, a job that releases expired holds.
PriceThe total shown in search is the total charged.A calculation rule shared by the search query and the payment command.
A failed stepIf the room has sold out, the traveller is told before paying and sees similar rooms.The refusal result, its precedence, and the screen that shows it.
DataBooking records are kept for seven years for tax.A data class with onErasure: retain, period: P7Y, and the job that erases afterwards.

Behavior points up

A use case names the journey step it serves (journey/book-a-room#s3) and the requirements it fulfills. Nothing in Intent names a use case, a screen or a state.

When code and Intent disagree

Where Intent has spoken, Intent wins: the code has a defect or the business must decide again. Where Intent is silent, working code is evidence for a question to the decider, never a source to copy into Intent unasked.

A page record

A Markdown file whose opening block, between two --- lines, is JSON naming the record's type and id. The prose below it holds the sections that type requires.

An entry record

An object inside the records array of a page. A requirement is an entry on a topic page; a term is an entry in docs/glossary.json.

An identifier

The type, a slash, then one to six lowercase words: goal/trusted-prices. A requirement adds its topic: requirement/checkout.shown-price-is-charged. A decision adds a number: decision/0001-room-hold-length.

Running litespec consumer in the published example passes on 40 files under docs/: project configuration, page frontmatter and glossary are valid.

Record Language: One Grammar for Names and Values

Every field name belongs to one of eight word classes, and every value follows the value style. The record language page states both for every layer, so a name learned on one record reads the same everywhere.

Word classReading testLantern fields
Identityfixed wordstype, id, title, owner, status
Link to many"journey serves goal"serves, fulfills, cites, reads
Link to one"the journey's actor"actor, fallback, result, host
Link acted on"acceptedBy the requirement"approvedBy, enforcedBy, triggeredBy
Closed set"its mode is atomic"kind, mode, lifecycle, level
Prose"its statement is ..."statement, rationale, failure
Conditioncompletes "when ..."finishedWhen, skippedWhen
Reasoncompletes "because ..."refusedBecause, appliesBecause

The value style, on Lantern's words

A statement ends in a period; a step starts with its actor; a meaning starts with A or An in 25 words; a rationale completes "because" without the word; a question ends in "?". Dates are ISO 8601, money is { amount, currency }, spelling is American (license, pseudonymize). The mechanical half is value-invalid.

Finding codes read the same way

Every code is <subject>-<problem>: requirement-cites-missing, constraint-unreached. Each message is one present-tense sentence of fact starting with the record's quoted identifier.

litespec/core/language#name-each-field-by-its-word-class and write-each-value-in-the-value-style. Only names an outside standard fixes are exempt: Gherkin given, when, then, and the CloudEvents attributes.

02

The Why

Product, actor, goal and problem: the records that say what the product is for, before anything is promised.

The why

Product: What Lantern Is, for Whom, and Where It Stops

Exactly one product record, kept in docs/intent/brief.md. It is the root of every record tree, and the first thing an agent reads before building anything.

What it is
One page that states the product's boundary, the actors it addresses, and its exclusions.
Why it exists
Every later choice is judged against it. exclusions gives a tempting expansion a written refusal to meet: when someone proposes "add flights", the brief already says no.
Write it
First, in one sitting with the decider. Keep it short; it changes rarely.
Checked by
product-count-invalid fails a second product record. litespec consumer fails a brief missing boundary, exclusions, addresses or its Summary section.
{
  "type": "product",
  "id": "product/lantern",
  "boundary": "Travellers find hotel rooms, book
     them and manage their bookings.",
  "exclusions": [
    "Lantern never sells flights or other transport.",
    "Lantern never stores a traveller's card details."
  ],
  "addresses": ["actor/traveller", "actor/hotel-partner"]
}
## Summary
Lantern lets travellers book a hotel room at the price
they were shown, and lets hotels fill rooms without a
call centre.
Notice how exclusions pays off later: because Lantern already refuses transport, the package travel constraint does not apply, and the constraint record can say so with applies: false.
The why

Actor: Who or What Touches the Product

An actor is a person, an organization or a system that uses Lantern or is affected by it. Actors are named once and then referenced everywhere else by identifier.

What it is
A page per actor with a kind (person, organization or system) and a Summary. Later, in Behavior, an actor holds roles that grant permissions.
Why it exists
Problems name who they hurt (affects), journeys name who walks them (actor), the product names who it is for (addresses). One record per actor keeps those three pointing at the same person.
Write one when
Someone appears in a problem, a journey, or the product's addresses.
Skip it when
The "actor" is really a role, such as "admin". Roles are Behavior records.

actor/hotel-partnerorganizationaddresses; sets its own free-cancellation window

The why

Goal: The Number Lantern Is Trying to Move

A goal is a measurable outcome in the world, not a thing the software does. It explains why features exist, and journeys walk toward it. A goal carries no authority: it never settles a conflict between two records.

What it is
A statement, what is counted (measure), the number aimed for (target), a date or milestone (dueBy), and a Why It Matters section.
Why it exists
Without a number nobody can tell whether a feature helped. With one, a cheaper feature that moves the same number can replace an expensive one.
Write one when
You can name what you would count. "Share of searches ending in a booking" is a goal; "a great booking experience" is not.
Skip it when
It describes the product's own behavior. "Checkout answers within 3 seconds" is a quality requirement, because Lantern controls it directly.
{
  "type": "goal",
  "id": "goal/more-completed-bookings",
  "statement": "More travellers who search for a
     room finish a booking.",
  "measure": "Share of searches that end in a
     confirmed booking",
  "target": "At least 4 percent",
  "dueBy": "Summer season 2027"
}
## Why It Matters
Every abandoned checkout is a traveller who found
what they wanted and still left. Lantern earns
nothing from a search.
The why

Problem: A Pain Someone Has Today

A problem describes the situation before Lantern fixes it, in the affected people's own terms, with evidence that it is real. It is the reason a feature exists.

What it is
A one-sentence statement, the actors it affects, and two sections: Situation (the pain, told as it happens) and Evidence (why we believe it).
Why it exists
Features without a problem attract work nobody needs. Writing the problem first also keeps options open: one problem usually admits several solutions.
Write one when
You can describe the pain without naming a solution, and you have evidence: tickets, interviews, numbers.
Skip it when
You can imagine only one way to solve it. Then you have written a feature in disguise; write the feature and the real problem behind it.
{
  "type": "problem",
  "id": "problem/price-surprises",
  "statement": "Travellers find fees at checkout
     that the search result did not show.",
  "affects": ["actor/traveller"]
}
## Situation
A traveller picks a room at EUR 120 a night and meets
city tax and a cleaning fee on the payment page.
## Evidence
In the pilot, 18 percent of support messages were
about a fee the traveller had not seen.
03

The What

Features say what Lantern offers and when; requirements say exactly what must hold. Requirement pages group them by topic.

The what

Goal, Problem, Feature, Requirement: Four Different Questions

These four look alike because each is a sentence about the product. They differ in what they are about and in who can change them. Read them as a chain from the world to the system, using one Lantern thread.

The test that separates them

Can Lantern's code make it true on its own? A requirement: yes. A goal: no, travellers decide. A problem: no, it is about the world. A feature: it is a decision about scope and timing.

Do you need all four?

A small project (size small) writes requirements without goals, problems, features or journeys. Those four switch on at size large, when scope and outcomes need tracking.

Can a requirement come straight from a goal?

Yes. cites holds any of goal, problem, feature, constraint, research and decision, in no order of precedence. The shown-price requirement cites its goal beside its feature and constraint.

The what

Feature: An Offer With a Lifecycle

A feature is a unit of scope the decider plans, ships, postpones or refuses. It names the problem it solves (its parent) and, unless refused, the goals it moves.

feature/room-hold shipped

solves rooms-sell-out-at-checkout
advances more-completed-bookings

Offering: the room a traveller starts to book is held while they pay. Out: holds without starting checkout.

feature/all-in-pricing planned

solves price-surprises
advances trusted-prices
plannedFor Launch

Offering: every price in search is the total for the stay. Out: currency conversion.

feature/pay-at-hotel refused

solves pay-before-stay (the problem it declines)
no advances

refusedBecause: Lantern would carry the no-show risk, which partners will not share.

Why a feature, if requirements already exist?
Requirements are permanent commitments. A feature is a roadmap fact with a state: planned, shipped, later or refused. It answers "what are we building next, and what have we said no to?"
Why record a refusal?
So nobody proposes pay-at-hotel again next quarter without meeting the reason it was refused.
How it affects checks
A requirement carried by a planned or later feature is not expected to be built yet, so requirement-fulfillment-missing skips it. Lantern's service fee is carried by the planned all-in pricing feature, and the check stays silent about it.
Checked by
litespec consumer: lifecycle, Offering, refusedBecause when refused, plannedFor when planned.
The what

Requirement: One Testable Commitment

A requirement is the smallest thing Lantern promises: one sentence that Behavior must fulfil and a test can prove. It is the record the rest of LiteSpec is built to keep.

kind: functional

What the product does.

"A room the traveller starts to book is held for them for 10 minutes."

kind: quality

How well it does it, as a measurable moment: stimulus, environment, response, measure, dueBy.

"During a flash sale, 95 percent of checkouts are answered within 3 seconds."

kind: policy

A value the business holds, with dated values.

"Lantern charges a service fee per booking": EUR 2.50 from 2027-01-01.

FieldRule
idrequirement/<topic>.<name>, the topic being its page
statementOne sentence a test can check
rationaleWhy it holds, in one line
citesWhere it came from: any goal, problem, feature, constraint, research or decision
measure, dueByBoth, whenever the statement names a number
except, fallbackWhen it does not hold, and what is promised instead
setBy, riskWho sets its value; what makes it tier 1
Write a good one
Name one observable outcome in product words. "The total shown in search is the total charged" passes. "Prices are transparent" fails: no test can check it.
Where it came from
Its parent is its topic page, by identifier containment. cites names its reasons; a requirement that cites nothing is requirement-cites-missing.
What happens next
Behavior records name it in fulfills. Until one does, requirement-fulfillment-missing reports it.
The what

Topic Page: Why Requirements Live Together

Requirements are small, and a reader needs their shared story to judge them. So LiteSpec keeps them as entries on one topic page per subject, which tells that story once in eight fixed sections and carries the topic record itself.

The chapter: the story, for people

Plus, in the frontmatter

The topic record: type: topic, id: topic/checkout, owner, status, reviewBy, and the records array with six requirement entries. The page lists no requirements in its prose; the book renders that list from the entries.

Path docs/intent/booking/checkout.md: the folder is the area (booking), the file the topic (checkout). The area is a folder only; the topic is the record, and the parent of its requirements.

The what

Except and Fallback: Every Exception Ends in a Promise

Sometimes a commitment cannot hold. Instead of leaving the traveller with nothing, the requirement names the condition in except and points to another requirement, the fallback, that covers what happens then.

Why one level deep
A chain of exceptions of exceptions is impossible to approve. One level means every exception ends in a promise the decider can read in the book, printed inline under the requirement.
Checked by
except-fallback-missing reports an except entry without its fallback.
The what

Quality Requirements and Policy Values

Two requirement kinds carry more than a sentence. A quality requirement describes one measurable moment. A policy requirement carries dated values, and setBy says whether the decider or each hotel sets them.

{ "id": "requirement/checkout.checkout-under-load",
  "kind": "quality",
  "stimulus": "2,000 travellers check out in the
     same minute during a flash sale",
  "environment": "Normal operation, one partner
     system answering slowly",
  "response": "Each booking is confirmed or refused",
  "measure": "Share of checkouts answered within
     3 seconds: at least 95 percent",
  "dueBy": "Summer season 2027" }

"Fast checkout" cannot be built or tested. This can: it says what arrives, under which conditions, what the product does, and how it is measured. The shape is the quality attribute scenario from software architecture practice.

{ "id": "requirement/checkout.service-fee",
  "kind": "policy", "setBy": "decider", "risk": ["money"],
  "values": [{ "value": "EUR 2.50",
               "effectiveFrom": "2027-01-01" }] }

{ "id": "requirement/checkout.free-cancellation-window",
  "kind": "policy", "setBy": "tenant",
  "statement": "Each hotel sets how long before arrival
     a booking can be cancelled free, 0 to 72 hours.",
  "values": [{ "value": "24 hours by default, 0 to 72 hours",
               "effectiveFrom": "2027-01-01" }] }

setBy: decider: the decider approves the figure. setBy: tenant: the decider approves the range and default once; each hotel's own choice inside it is data, never a ballot.

04

The Outside World

Constraints bind, research informs, decisions choose. Together they explain where a requirement came from and what would change it.

The outside world

Constraint: An Outside Rule That Binds Lantern

A constraint is a rule Lantern did not choose: a law, a regulation, a provider's terms. It outranks everything else in the project. Recording it, even when it does not apply, shows the team asked the question.

{ "id": "constraint/total-price-display",
  "authority": "EU consumer law on price indication",
  "kind": "legal",
  "applies": true,
  "appliesBecause": "Lantern sells to consumers
     in the EU." }
## Summary  A price shown to a consumer includes every
             unavoidable tax and fee.
## What It Requires  generated: requirement/checkout.
                      shown-price-is-charged
{ "id": "constraint/package-travel-rules",
  "applies": false,
  "appliesBecause": "Lantern sells hotel stays only
     and never combines them with transport." }
What it is
The rule's source (authority), a kind, whether it applies and why, and two sections: the rule in its source's words, and what the product does to obey it.
Why record one that does not apply
A later reader can tell "we checked package travel law, it does not apply because of exclusions" from "nobody thought of it".
Write one when
Someone outside Lantern can hold it to the rule: a regulator, a card scheme, a partner contract.
Skip it when
It is Lantern's own choice. "Holds last 10 minutes" is a decision, not a constraint.
Generated answer
What It Requires is a generated region listing the requirements that cite the constraint. A constraint with applies: true that none cites is constraint-unreached.
The outside world

From a Constraint to Running Code

A constraint does nothing on its own. It becomes actionable through a requirement that cites it, which Behavior fulfils, which code implements, which a test proves. Each arrow is a link someone writes.

What 3.0.0 closed

A constraint with applies: true and no citing requirement is constraint-unreached, and What It Requires renders from the citing requirements, so prose and links cannot drift.

Research follows the same chain

A requirement may cite research in cites the same way. The difference is authority: a constraint binds; research only informs.

The outside world

Research: A Fact From the World, With Its Date

Research records something Lantern learned outside its own code: a provider's limit, a market number, a regulator's guidance. It carries no authority, but every choice built on it can be rechecked when the source changes.

What it is
The question asked, a one-sentence conclusion, the sources with the date each was read, and an Answer section that argues claim by claim.
Why it exists
A fact without a source and a date cannot be rechecked. When a partner changes its API, Lantern knows exactly which decisions rested on the old limit.
Write one when
A decision or requirement depends on something you did not decide yourself, and that could change.
Skip it when
The fact binds you. A partner contract clause is a constraint; a partner's observed behavior is research.
Agent output
Reports an agent generates outside docs/ are source material with no authority until a research record or an approved decision restates the claim.
{
  "type": "research",
  "id": "research/partner-hold-limits",
  "question": "How long will hotel partners hold
     a room without payment?",
  "conclusion": "Partner systems hold a room for at
     most 15 minutes without payment, so checkout
     must finish inside that window.",
  "sources": [
    { "url": "https://example.com/partner-api/holds",
      "readOn": "2026-10-12" }
  ]
}
## Answer
All three pilot partners release an unpaid hold after
15 minutes; one releases it after 12. High confidence
for the pilot partners, unknown for others.
The outside world

Decision: A Choice Between Options, Replaced Never Edited

A decision records a choice the business made: the options, what spoke for and against each, what was chosen, and the condition that would reopen it. When the choice changes, a new decision replaces the old one.

{
  "type": "decision",
  "id": "decision/0001-room-hold-length",
  "options": [
    { "name": "10 minutes",
      "pros": "Leaves a buffer before the shortest
             partner limit.",
      "cons": "Slow payers may lose the room." },
    { "name": "15 minutes",
      "pros": "Most time for the traveller.",
      "cons": "Equals the partner limit, so a slow
             partner call loses the room after payment." }
  ],
  "reopenedWhen": "A partner holds rooms longer than 15
     minutes, or more than 5 percent of checkouts end
     because the hold ran out.",
  "decides": ["requirement/checkout.room-held-during-checkout"],
  "basedOn": ["research/partner-hold-limits"]
}
## Context  ## Decision  ## Consequences
Why it exists
A requirement says "10 minutes"; only the decision says why not 15. Without it, the next person reopens the question from scratch.
Why replace, not edit
Editing loses the reasoning that once held. A new decision names the old one in replaces, and the old one is retired in the same change, so the history stays readable.
reopenedWhen
A condition, not a date: the decision stays until the world actually changes.
On approval
choice names the option, and approvedBy, approvedOn and approvedHash seal it. Lantern's decision is still a draft.
Product or technical
This one lives in docs/intent/decisions/. A technical decision, such as the database, is the same type under docs/implementation/decisions/.
05

The Walk

Journeys show whether a person can actually get from start to finish. The glossary keeps the words straight.

The walk

Journey: Can Ana Actually Book a Room?

Requirements are separate sentences. None of them shows whether a traveller can get from search to confirmation. A journey does: ordered steps, each one product sentence, walking one actor toward one goal.

{
  "type": "journey",
  "id": "journey/book-a-room",
  "actor": "actor/traveller",
  "serves": ["goal/more-completed-bookings"],
  "finishedWhen": "The traveller holds a confirmation
     and the hotel has the booking.",
  "cites": ["requirement/checkout.room-held-...", ...],
  "steps": [
    { "id": "s1", "statement": "The traveller
       searches for rooms by city and dates." },
    { "id": "s2", "statement": "... compares rooms
       by their total price for the stay." },
    { "id": "s3", "statement": "... chooses a room
       and starts to book it.",
      "failure": "If the room has sold out, the
        traveller is told before paying and sees
        similar rooms." },
    { "id": "s4", "statement": "... pays
       for the booking." },
    { "id": "s5", "statement": "... receives the
       confirmation, and the hotel receives the booking." } ] }
Why journeys exist
They let the decider approve an experience in plain sentences, and they give Behavior a target. A use case names the step it serves, journey/book-a-room#s3, so the checks can show every step has a use case, a screen and a passing test.
Write one when
A person's task crosses several actions, and a gap between them would strand them: book, pay, confirm.
Skip it when
It is one action, such as "change my email". A requirement is enough.
The rules
One actor toward one goal; a step names another actor only at a handoff. Each step has an id and a statement, and names no use case. failure says what the person experiences, never an error code. Body sections: Example and Between the Steps.
Staff journeys
The same shape for employees: a support agent fixing a failed booking.
The walk

What the Decider Reads: Lantern's Journey in the Book

The book is the reader LiteSpec generates from the records. It shows the journey with no field names and no JSON. The decider approves the promises, or answers the open questions, from this view alone.

Book a Room
Finished when: the traveller holds a confirmation and the hotel has the booking.

1. The traveller searches for rooms by city and dates.
2. The traveller compares rooms by their total price for the stay.
3. The traveller chooses a room and starts to book it.
   If it goes wrong: if the room has sold out, the traveller is told before paying and sees similar rooms.
4. The traveller pays for the booking.
   If it goes wrong: if the payment fails, the room stays held until the 10 minutes end.
5. The traveller receives the confirmation, and the hotel receives the booking.

Promises
- A room the traveller starts to book is held for them for 10 minutes.
    Except when the hotel partner refuses to hold the room.
    Then a traveller is never charged for a room that was not confirmed.
- The total shown in search is the total charged.

Consequences context only

Rendered from Behavior once it exists: new refusals in product words ("This room is no longer available"), personal data kept and for how long, money effects. Outside what the decider approves.

Deciding and Approving

How a draft becomes approved, how agents ask and the decider answers, who decides which figure, and what wins a conflict.

Deciding and approving

Approval Is Data, Sealed by a Hash

An Intent record moves from draft to approved to retired. Approval is written on the record itself, and a hash over its meaningful fields reopens the approval whenever the meaning changes.

Inside the hash

Fields that change meaning: Lantern's statement "held for 10 minutes", the steps, the except entries.

Outside the hash

Prose sections, translated labels, the consequences box. Fixing a typo in Situation reopens nothing.

Parent reopened

When the decider changes the hold to 15 minutes, records built on it are flagged by parent-reopened until they are updated.

Deciding and approving

Questions and Ballots: How Agents Ask and the Decider Answers

When an agent needs a figure Intent does not state, it does not guess. It adds a draft question to the record, with a recommendation. Questions on one topic share a ballot, so the decider answers them together.

Deciding and approving

Who Decides Which Figure

Agents add detail freely, but some figures are business choices only an accountable person may make. LiteSpec names them, so an agent raises a question instead of quietly writing a number into the design.

The decider decides

Any figure that is a

  • price, fee, rate or term (the EUR 2.50 service fee)
  • market or payer
  • personal-data retention period
  • freshness a person can observe
  • default that varies by tenant, market or date

Each hotel sets, as data

A setBy: tenant value is chosen by each tenant inside the range the decider approved. Lantern's hotels choose their free-cancellation window between 0 and 72 hours. Hotel A picking 48 hours needs no ballot.

Agents decide

Everything else Behavior needs: states, rules, refusals, how a hold is released, what is retried. These are reviewed by developers, not the decider.

The founder is one decider

Records say decider, one name from the project's owner list. In Lantern it is the founder; in a larger company it is whoever owns the commercial call.

Incidents

A production fix at 02:00 may ship before anyone answers. It carries a time-boxed incident exception that becomes a draft question within seven days.

Deciding and approving

Authority: Which Record Wins a Conflict

When two records disagree, the higher one wins. Lantern example: the consumer-law constraint says the shown price includes every fee; if a use case added a fee at payment, the constraint wins and the use case is wrong.

Between two decisions

The later approved one wins, and its replaces link shows what it overturned.

No authority

Goals, problems, features, journeys, research and prose explain and connect; they never override an approved record.

Code versus records

Where Intent has spoken, it wins. Where it is silent, working code is evidence for a question.

Exception

A departure outranks the LiteSpec provision its covers names, for that project, until its expiresOn date.

Deciding and approving

Change Moves Both Ways Through Lantern

Promises flow down and are updated when they change. Questions and facts from the world flow up. Nothing flows up from code: a surprise in production enters Intent as research, never as a copied behavior.

Top-down: the decider changes a promise

  1. The decider answers the ballot with 15 minutes instead of the recommended 10.
  2. A new decision replaces decision/0001-room-hold-length; the old one is retired.
  3. The requirement's statement changes, so its approval reopens.
  4. An agent updates the hold rule and its scenario in Behavior.
  5. A developer reviews a two-line Behavior change; tests pass; the book shows the new promise.

Bottom-up: the world surprises Lantern

  1. A traveller is charged but loses the room: one partner now releases holds after 8 minutes.
  2. An agent records this as research, with the source and the date observed.
  3. The new fact undercuts the research the 10-minute decision was based on (12 to 15 minutes).
  4. The decider sees one tier-1 question: shorten holds, or drop that partner?
  5. On approval, Behavior follows. The code was never the source.
